# Pain Point and Root Cause Analysis

## SIH Problem Statement ID
26133

---

## Purpose

This document analyzes the major pain points in rural and underserved public healthcare services. For each pain point, it identifies the affected users, root causes, current impact and possible solution opportunities.

---

## Pain Point Analysis

| No. | Pain Point | Who Is Affected | Root Cause | Current Impact | Solution Opportunity |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| 1 | Long travel distances to healthcare facilities | Patients, caregivers, especially rural residents | Healthcare facilities and specialists are concentrated far from some communities | Increased travel cost and time, delayed consultation, missed care | Assisted teleconsultation, facility/service visibility, and better referral coordination |
| 2 | Shortage of doctors and specialists | Patients, frontline healthcare workers, PHCs | Limited availability and uneven distribution of healthcare professionals | Delayed access to expert consultation and increased workload at primary facilities | Assisted teleconsultation and structured coordination with available doctors and specialists |
| 3 | Delayed consultation and long waiting time | Patients, caregivers, healthcare staff | Limited staff, inefficient patient flow and lack of structured prioritization | Patients wait longer and urgent cases may not receive timely attention | Digital triage, appointment scheduling and queue management |
| 4 | Fragmented medical records | Patients, doctors, healthcare facilities | Patient information does not consistently follow the patient across different facilities | Repeated information collection and poor continuity of care | Longitudinal digital patient records accessible across the healthcare journey |
| 5 | Delayed or incomplete referrals | Patients, frontline workers, doctors, hospitals | Referrals may lack structured tracking and coordination between facilities | Patients may experience delays or fail to complete required referrals | Closed-loop referral creation, status tracking and referral completion monitoring |
| 6 | Diagnostic coordination problems | Patients, doctors, frontline workers, laboratory staff | Limited visibility and coordination of diagnostic services and test requirements | Delays in diagnosis, repeated visits and difficulty tracking required tests | Diagnostic coordination module with test status and service availability visibility |
| 7 | Limited medicine availability visibility | Patients, healthcare workers, pharmacists | Healthcare workers and patients may not have timely information about medicine availability | Unnecessary travel, treatment delays and difficulty planning patient care | Medicine availability visibility and facility-level medicine status tracking |
| 8 | Missed follow-ups for high-risk patients | Maternal patients, children, chronic condition patients, frontline healthcare workers | Lack of structured follow-up scheduling, tracking and reminders | Important follow-up visits may be delayed or missed | High-risk patient identification, follow-up scheduling and tracking |
| 9 | Low or unreliable internet connectivity | Rural patients, frontline healthcare workers and healthcare facilities | Remote locations may have poor network infrastructure | Digital systems may become unavailable or difficult to use | Offline-first data capture, local storage and synchronization when connectivity is available |
| 10 | Language and health literacy barriers | Patients, caregivers and frontline healthcare workers | Users may speak different languages and have varying levels of health literacy | Misunderstanding of healthcare information and reduced adoption of digital systems | Multilingual interface, simple language and assisted interaction |
| 11 | Limited visibility for healthcare administrators | Healthcare administrators and facility managers | Healthcare information and operational data may be scattered across different processes | Difficulty monitoring service quality, patient flow, referrals and follow-ups | Facility dashboards with key healthcare and operational indicators |
| 12 | Poor continuity of care between healthcare facilities | Patients, doctors, frontline workers and hospitals | Patient information, referrals and treatment progress may not move consistently between facilities | Repeated assessments, information loss and delayed treatment | Integrated longitudinal records and coordinated patient journey tracking |
| 13 | Difficulty identifying and escalating emergency or high-risk cases | Patients, frontline healthcare workers and doctors | Lack of structured prioritization and clear escalation workflows | Urgent patients may experience delays in receiving appropriate attention | Digital triage with high-risk alerts and emergency escalation workflows |
| 14 | Limited access due to affordability and operational barriers | Patients and caregivers | Travel costs, repeated facility visits and delays increase the overall burden of accessing healthcare | Patients may delay or avoid necessary healthcare services | Reduced unnecessary travel, better service coordination and early access to appropriate care |

---

## Key Analysis Conclusion

The pain points identified above show that the challenge is not limited to a single healthcare service such as teleconsultation. The main problem is the lack of continuity and coordination throughout the complete patient healthcare journey.

The proposed solution should therefore connect the major stages of care, including patient registration, symptom and vital information collection, patient prioritization, consultation support, longitudinal records, referral coordination and tracking, follow-up management, and facility-level monitoring.

The solution should especially focus on reducing unnecessary travel and delays, improving continuity of patient information, supporting frontline healthcare workers, identifying high-risk cases earlier, and strengthening coordination between different levels of the existing public healthcare system.
